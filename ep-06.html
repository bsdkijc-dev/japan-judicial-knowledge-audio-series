Mastering Civil Preparatory Proceedings: Japan's Pre-Trial Issue Clarification Framework

By Cecep Mustafa, Indonesian Judicial College (IJC) · ORCID 0000-0003-0037-497X

About This Episode

In this episode, Cecep Mustafa analyzes Japan's civil preparatory proceedings (kōzō chōsei tetsuzuki) under the Code of Civil Procedure (Act No. 109 of 1996) and evaluates their strategic value for civil procedural reform in Indonesia. He examines how Japanese courts use the statutory Right of Clarification (shakumeiken), binding Trial Plans (shinkō keikaku), and inter-party inquiries to identify disputed issues, organize evidence, and improve procedural efficiency before trial. Cecep Mustafa then compares Japanese practice with Indonesia's HIR/RBg framework and PTUN administrative procedures, highlighting opportunities to strengthen pre-trial case preparation. The episode presents a structured reform blueprint for introducing mandatory Civil Preparatory Examinations (Pemeriksaan Persiapan Perdata) in Indonesian District Courts, with a focus on issue identification, evidence management, procedural clarity, judicial case management, and more efficient trials.

Why This Topic Matters

The efficiency of civil adjudication depends largely on how effectively courts and litigants structure legal claims and evidence during the pre-trial phase. Without mandatory pre-trial issue clarification, civil lawsuits often enter trial with vague pleadings, surprise evidence submissions, and unfocused witness hearings. This results in repeated schedule postponements, prolonged litigation, and low settlement rates. Examining Japan's front-loaded pre-trial framework provides actionable insights for shifting civil litigation from passive administrative processing to active judicial management.

Japan Perspective

Civil litigation in Japan is governed by the Code of Civil Procedure (Act No. 109 of 1996). The 1996 Code reform structurally integrated issue and evidence arrangement proceedings (kozo chosei tetuzuki) into ordinary civil procedure through three statutory options: Preliminary Oral Arguments under Articles 164 through 167, Preparatory Proceedings under Articles 168 through 174, and Written Preparatory Proceedings under Articles 175 through 178.

Active judicial management is grounded in the Presiding Judge's statutory Right of Clarification (shakumeiken) under Article 149. This authority empowers courts to question litigants, require pleading revisions, and order the production of documentary evidence under Article 151. Courts also establish binding Trial Plans (shinkou keikaku) under Article 147-2 to set fixed schedules for issue organization and witness testimony.

To gather necessary facts early, litigants utilize Pre-Filing Inquiries under Article 132-2, Pre-Filing Evidence Collection under Article 132-4, and Inter-Party Inquiries under Article 163. Once preparatory proceedings conclude, Articles 157, 167, 174, and 178 enforce strict preclusion rules, dismissing late allegations or evidence submitted without reasonable justification. Under the Act on Promotion of Speedier Trials (Act No. 107 of 2003), Japanese first-instance proceedings target completion within two years, achieving an actual average trial duration of 9 to 14 months. Furthermore, integrated in-court settlement discussions (Wakai) under Article 89 yield settlement rates between 36 percent and 37.1 percent.

COMPARATIVE PERSPECTIVE A comparative analysis reveals fundamental structural differences between civil procedure in Japan and Indonesia.

In Indonesia, standard civil litigation in District Courts (Pengadilan Negeri) is governed by legacy colonial codes: the Revised Inlandsch Reglement (HIR) and the Reglement tot Regeling van het Rechtswezen in de Buitengewesten (RBg). The HIR/RBg framework lacks a general, mandatory pre-trial issue clarification stage. Civil suits proceed directly from initial appearances to court-annexed mediation under PERMA No. 1 of 2016, followed immediately by serial written exchanges (jawaban, replik, duplik) and piecemeal evidence presentation in open court.

However, specific statutory sub-jurisdictions in Indonesia demonstrate the viability of active pre-trial management. Administrative Courts (Pengadilan Tata Usaha Negara / PTUN) under Article 63 of Law No. 5 of 1986 mandate a Preparatory Examination (Pemeriksaan Persiapan), granting judges ex-officio authority to order pleading revisions within 30 days and compel administrative document production. Similarly, Small Claims Courts (Gugatan Sederhana) under PERMA No. 2 of 2015 and PERMA No. 4 of 2019 utilize a Preliminary Examination (Pemeriksaan Pendahuluan) by a single judge to verify claim eligibility for monetary claims under Rp 500,000,000. Additionally, PERMA No. 7 of 2022 provides an electronic framework (E-Court and E-Litigasi) for digital document transmission, though it lacks interactive pre-trial issue framing features.

These structural disparities generate contrasting operational metrics. In Indonesian general civil courts, the absence of early issue clarification leads to high rates of interlocutory exceptions for vague pleadings (obscuur libel, occurring in 64.5 percent of cases) and prolonged trial durations averaging 7.5 months despite the 5-month administrative target under SEMA No. 2 of 2014. Mediation success in general civil cases remains low, ranging between 1.4 percent and 5 percent, because parties negotiate before evidence is organized. In Japan, front-loading case preparation yields predictable 9 to 14 month disposition timelines and in-court settlement rates exceeding 36 percent.

Observe → Understand → Compare → Adapt → Pilot → Improve

The methodology for adapting Japanese pre-trial practices into Indonesian civil procedure follows six structured steps:

Observe: Identify systemic trial delays in Indonesian District Courts caused by vague pleadings (obscuur libel), surprise evidence submissions, and passive pre-trial judicial involvement.

Understand: Recognize that Japan achieves civil trial efficiency through statutory issue arrangement proceedings, the Right of Clarification (shakumeiken), and binding procedural preclusion.

Compare: Contrast Indonesia's passive HIR/RBg pre-trial structure with Japan's active issue arrangement under Code of Civil Procedure Articles 164 through 178, while noting existing Indonesian precedents in PTUN Law Article 63 and PERMA No. 4 of 2019.

Adapt: Formulate specific regulatory proposals for Mahkamah Agung under a new Supreme Court Regulation (PERMA) on Civil Preparatory Proceedings (Pemeriksaan Persiapan Perdata / PPP):

Codify judicial ex-officio clarification powers (Hak Menjelaskan) to order pleading rectifications and issue binding Pre-Trial Issue Summaries (Risalah Sengketa) within 14 days.

Establish a Pre-Trial Digital Evidence Disclosure Protocol (PT-DEDP) within E-Court, requiring advance document exchange and joint issue matrices prior to scheduling witness hearings.

Implement a Dual-Track Settlement Mechanism allowing trial judges to conduct Judicial Settlement Conferences post-issue clarification, leveraging factual summaries to guide discussions.

Enforce statutory preclusion rules that forfeit a party's right to introduce late claims or evidence after the preparatory phase closes.

Pilot: Deploy a 90-day pilot program across five Class IA Special District Courts: Central Jakarta, Surabaya, Medan, Bandung, and Makassar.

Improve: Measure pilot performance against clear quantitative benchmarks:

Reduce average first-instance trial duration from 7.5 months to under 4.5 months.

Increase SEMA No. 2 of 2014 5-month target compliance from 58.2 percent to over 92.0 percent.

Raise commercial in-court settlement success rates from 4.1 percent to over 25.0 percent.

Reduce interlocutory exceptions for vague pleadings (obscuur libel) from 64.5 percent to under 15.0 percent of cases.

Decrease average evidentiary trial sessions from 11.4 sessions to under 5.0 sessions per case.

Judicial Knowledge Takeaways

Active Judicial Clarification Prevents Trial Detours: Empowering judges to question litigants and order pleading rectifications early eliminates vague claims (obscuur libel) before trial.

Pre-Trial Evidence Disclosure Eliminates Surprise Submissions: Requiring mutual document exchange prior to witness hearings prevents unexpected postponements and focuses oral testimony.

Binding Procedural Calendars Enforce Trial Discipline: Establishing fixed schedules with strict preclusion rules for late evidence ensures compliance with statutory disposition targets.

Post-Clarification Settlement Increases Resolution Rates: Facilitating settlement discussions after issues and evidence are clarified provides litigants with a realistic basis for compromise.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the Presiding Judge's Right of Clarification (shakumeiken) in Japan? A: Grounded in Article 149 of the Japanese Code of Civil Procedure, the Right of Clarification authorizes the presiding judge to ask questions, request pleading revisions, and order parties to produce evidence on or outside scheduled hearing dates.

Q: Does Indonesian law contain any existing examples of pre-trial preparatory examinations? A: Yes. Article 63 of Law No. 5 of 1986 governing Administrative Courts (PTUN) mandates a Preparatory Examination (Pemeriksaan Persiapan), and PERMA No. 4 of 2019 governing Small Claims Courts includes a single-judge Preliminary Examination (Pemeriksaan Pendahuluan).

Q: What is the proposed Civil Preparatory Examination (Pemeriksaan Persiapan Perdata) for Indonesian District Courts? A: It is a proposed mandatory pre-trial phase conducted by judges in District Courts to review pleadings, enforce digital evidence disclosure, issue binding Pre-Trial Issue Summaries (Risalah Sengketa), and conduct post-clarification settlement conferences.

Q: How does Japan handle evidence submitted late in civil proceedings? A: Under Articles 157, 157-2, 167, 174, and 178 of the Japanese Code of Civil Procedure, courts have statutory authority to dismiss allegations or evidence submitted late due to intent or gross negligence after preparatory proceedings close.

Q: What performance targets are set for the 90-day Civil Preparatory Examination pilot in Indonesia? A: Targets include reducing average trial duration to under 4.5 months, increasing SEMA 2/2014 timeline compliance above 92 percent, raising commercial settlement rates above 25 percent, and reducing vague pleading exceptions below 15 percent.
