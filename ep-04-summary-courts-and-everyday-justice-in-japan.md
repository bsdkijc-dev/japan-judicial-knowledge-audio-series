## About This Episode

In this episode, Cecep Mustafa examines Japan's judicial education architecture centered at the Legal Training and Research Institute (Shihō Kenshūjo) under the Supreme Court of Japan. He analyzes how structured clinical apprenticeships, co-mediation simulation modules, and periodic jurisprudence seminars can develop neutral facilitation competencies, practical courtroom skills, and reflective judicial practice. Cecep Mustafa then compares Japan's judicial training approach with Indonesia's mediator certification framework under Supreme Court Regulation (PERMA) No. 1 of 2016 and SK KMA No. 117/KMA/SKA/IV/2018. The episode presents a practical blueprint for clinical judicial education reform at BSDK Kumdil, emphasizing experiential learning, simulation-based training, continuous professional development, standardized competencies, and evidence-based evaluation of judicial mediation practice.

## Why This Topic Matters

The quality of judicial dispute resolution depends directly on the professional competence and ethical standards of judicial personnel. While procedural regulations establish formal frameworks for court-annexed mediation, facilitation skills cannot be acquired solely through lecture-based instruction in statutory norms. Examining how Japan structures clinical judicial education provides valuable strategies for moving beyond basic 40-hour administrative certification courses toward continuous, simulation-driven judicial development.

## Japan Perspective

Judicial education in Japan is centralized under the Supreme Court of Japan at the Legal Training and Research Institute (Shihō Kenshūjo). The institute oversees induction training for legal apprentices (shūshūsei) who have passed the National Bar Examination, as well as continuing professional education for appointed career judges (saibankan).

The curriculum at Shihō Kenshūjo combines theoretical analysis of civil procedure with practical clinical apprenticeships in courtrooms. For civil conciliation (Minji Chōtei), training emphasizes interest-based negotiation, psychological de-escalation, and preliminary evidentiary analysis (sōten seiri). Career judges designated as Conciliation Judges (Chōtei-kan) receive specialized instruction in supervising lay Conciliation Commissioners (Chōtei-iin) and managing multi-member panel dynamics.

Continuing education is maintained through structured seminars organized by High Courts and the National Conciliation Association Federation (Nihon Chōtei Kyōkai Rengōkai). These programs utilize casebooks (Chōteiin Hikkei), mock conciliation sessions (mock conciliation), and peer record audits to ensure uniform facilitation standards across all judicial districts.

COMPARATIVE PERSPECTIVE A comparative assessment reveals significant differences in judicial education structures between Japan and Indonesia.

In Indonesia, judicial education and mediator certification are managed by the Judicial Policy Strategy and Training Body (BSDK Kumdil) through its Technical Judicial Training Center (Pusdiklat Teknis Peradilan). Active career judges complete certified mediation courses conducted by BSDK. For non-judge mediators, certification is provided by university centers and private training institutes accredited under SK KMA No. 117/KMA/SKA/IV/2018, requiring a minimum 40-classroom-hour curriculum.

Key operational differences include:

First, Indonesia's 40-hour certification course focuses primarily on normative legal lectures and basic role-play exercises. The system lacks mandatory clinical courtroom apprenticeships or post-certification supervised practice prior to court roster placement.

Second, Indonesia maintains no statutory requirement for periodic re-certification or performance-based portfolio audits for accredited non-judge mediators. Once certified, mediators remain on court rosters without systematic quality oversight.

Third, training for judge mediators does not cover co-mediation dynamics (co-mediation dynamic) with external technical experts, leaving career judges unprepared to lead hybrid panels.

In contrast, Japan's Shihō Kenshūjo embeds multi-month clinical apprenticeships into core legal training and maintains continuous professional development through regional judicial associations.

Observe → Understand → Compare → Adapt → Pilot → Improve

The operational methodology for adapting Japanese judicial training insights into Indonesian court reform follows six structured stages:

Observe: Identify skill deficits in Indonesian court mediation, where 40-hour lecture courses leave mediators underprepared for complex commercial or land disputes.

Understand: Recognize that Japanese conciliation quality is sustained through Shihō Kenshūjo clinical apprenticeships, standardized casebooks, and continuous peer review.

Compare: Contrast Indonesia's static 40-hour certification model against Japan's integrated clinical training and lifelong judicial education framework.

Adapt: Formulate specific training reform proposals under the JPAIS-MED-TRN-001 framework for BSDK Kumdil:

Restructure the 40-hour certification curriculum to include 20 hours of clinical case simulations based on actual court dossiers.

Mandate a 30-day clinical court apprenticeship (pemagangan klinis) at first-instance courts before roster placement.

Introduce a specialized curriculum on co-mediation dynamics for judge mediators and external expert commissioners.

Enact a mandatory 3-year re-certification requirement (mandatory re-certification) evaluated through settlement portfolio audits.

Pilot: Launch the revised clinical training module at BSDK Kumdil for 60 judge mediators and 30 external expert candidates serving pilot courts.

Improve: Evaluate training efficacy against participant procedural justice satisfaction scores (target PJSS >= 4.30 out of 5.00) and initial pilot court settlement conversion rates.

## Judicial Knowledge Takeaways

Clinical Apprenticeships Build Practical Facilitation Skills: Courtroom apprenticeships under experienced mentors prepare trainees for complex dispute dynamics far more effectively than classroom lectures.

Co-Mediation Training Enables Hybrid Panel Success: Instructing judges and technical experts in joint panel facilitation resolves role ambiguity and improves negotiation leadership.

Mandatory Re-Certification Sustains Professional Standards: Requiring periodic re-certification based on portfolio audits removes inactive or underperforming neutrals from official court rosters.

Standardized Benchbooks Ensure National Consistency: Publishing detailed operational handbooks maintains uniform ethical and procedural standards across regional court jurisdictions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What institution manages judicial education in Japan? A: Judicial education in Japan is centralized at the Legal Training and Research Institute (Shihō Kenshūjo), an organ of the Supreme Court of Japan.

Q: How is non-judge mediator certification structured in Indonesia? A: Non-judge mediator certification in Indonesia requires a minimum 40-hour course delivered by training organizations accredited by the Supreme Court under SK KMA No. 117/KMA/SKA/IV/2018.

Q: What is the main limitation of Indonesia's current mediator training model? A: The current model relies heavily on theoretical lectures without requiring clinical courtroom apprenticeships, supervised practice, or periodic re-certification.

Q: What is the JPAIS-MED-TRN-001 training proposal? A: JPAIS-MED-TRN-001 is a proposed BSDK Kumdil curriculum reform introducing 20 hours of clinical case simulations, a 30-day court apprenticeship, co-mediation instruction, and a 3-year re-certification requirement.

Q: How does Japan maintain quality control among lay conciliation commissioners? A: Japan maintains quality control through regional High Court seminars, the National Conciliation Association Federation, standardized handbooks (Chōteiin Hikkei), and regular peer reviews.
