## Why This Topic Matters

Modernizing court case management through digital technology is essential for reducing litigation costs, eliminating procedural delays, and enhancing public transparency. However, executing digital transformation solely through decentralized administrative decrees can create fragmented database topologies, synchronization failures, and data privacy vulnerabilities. Analyzing Japan's statutory civil digitalization provides actionable insights for shifting court IT from isolated local software tools to a centralized, semantically structured, and constitutionally resilient judicial cloud architecture.

## Japan Perspective

Japan's civil court digitalization is anchored in primary parliamentary legislation enacted through the Code of Civil Procedure Amendment (Act No. 48 of 2022) and the Comprehensive Digitalization Integration Act of June 2023. The statutory roadmap establishes full enforcement by May 21, 2026, structured around the "Three Pillars of Civil Procedure Digitalization" ("3e" framework).

The first pillar, e-Court, codifies remote proceedings under Code of Civil Procedure Article 87-2 for oral arguments and Article 179-3 for preparatory proceedings. To uphold the open-court doctrine under Article 82 of the Constitution of Japan, presiding judges sit in physical courtrooms where remote video feeds of litigating parties are projected onto public gallery monitors.

The second pillar, e-Filing, centers on the mints platform (MINji saiban shorui denshi Teishutsu System), operated directly by the Supreme Court of Japan (Saikō Saibansho). Electronic submission via mints is mandatory for approximately 30,000 licensed attorneys. To protect access to justice under Article 32 of the Constitution, unrepresented (pro-se) litigants retain the right to submit paper pleadings, which court clerical staff scan and ingest directly into the digital record.

The third pillar, e-Case Management, replaces physical dossier transmission across Summary Courts, District Courts, High Courts, and the Supreme Court with a centralized cloud repository. Authenticated parties inspect digital dockets around the clock, while third parties inspect public records at dedicated courthouse viewing terminals enforcing automated redaction under the Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI).

COMPARATIVE PERSPECTIVE A comparative analysis between Japan and Indonesia highlights contrasting legal bases, database architectures, and operational safeguards.

In Indonesia, judicial digitalization has been driven by autonomous rulemaking (regelende bevoegdheid) under Supreme Court Regulations, including PERMA No. 1 of 2019 and PERMA No. 7 of 2022. The system encompasses e-Filing, e-Payment, e-Summons, and e-Litigasi within the e-Court portal, alongside the Case Tracking Information System (Sistem Informasi Penelusuran Perkara / SIPP) across more than 800 working units, and e-Berpadu for criminal case management. In 2024, first-instance courts registered 410,754 civil, religious, and administrative cases through e-Court.

However, significant structural differences exist across core technical dimensions:

First, Indonesia operates a decentralized database network where each court manages local relational database instances (MySQL or MariaDB) that transmit nightly batch updates to central servers. In remote island circuits (daerah 3T), telecommunications disruptions cause synchronization lag, data mismatches, and registry lockouts. Japan operates a single centralized cloud repository connecting all 438 Summary Courts, 50 District Courts, 8 High Courts, and the Supreme Court over a secure private intranet.

Second, Indonesia's e-Court requires unrepresented litigants to navigate complex multi-factor digital identity onboarding, creating procedural hurdles at courthouse Integrated One-Stop Service (Pelayanan Terpadu Satu Pintu / PTSP) desks. Japan enforces a statutory dual-track safeguard where attorney e-Filing is mandatory but paper filings for pro-se citizens remain legally protected and digitized by court staff.

Third, Indonesian court repositories store documents as unstructured flat PDFs and DOCX files without semantic XML markup, leaving records vulnerable to automated scraper botnets and unredacted Personal Identity Number (Nomor Induk Kependudukan / NIK) leaks under Law No. 27 of 2022 on Personal Data Protection (PDP Law). Japan utilizes structured PDF formats with XML registry wrappers and enforces strict public key infrastructure (PKI) encryption.

Observe → Understand → Compare → Adapt → Pilot → Improve

The operational methodology for adapting Japanese digital court practices into Indonesian judicial reform follows six structured stages:

Observe: Identify technical vulnerabilities in Indonesia's digital court ecosystem, including SIPP asynchronous batch synchronization failures, unstructured binary PDF storage, and PII exposure under the PDP Law.

Understand: Recognize that Japan achieves high-integrity digital justice by anchoring reforms in primary parliamentary statutes, centralizing cloud records, and maintaining dual-track pro-se access safeguards.

Compare: Contrast Indonesia's decentralized SIPP local databases and autonomous PERMA decrees against Japan's centralized mints cloud repository and Code of Civil Procedure Act No. 48 of 2022 amendments.

Adapt: Formulate the e-Peradilan Modern reference architecture for Mahkamah Agung:

Anchor digital mandates directly in primary legislation via the upcoming national Civil Procedure Code revision (RUU Hukum Acara Perdata).

Transition SIPP from local relational databases to a high-availability, multi-tenant PostgreSQL central cloud repository with Apache Kafka event streaming.

Adopt the OASIS LegalDocML (Akoma Ntoso) standard to convert flat PDFs into machine-readable legal XML schemas.

Deploy automated PDP-compliant PII redaction microservices and BSrE-BSSN hardware security module (HSM) digital signatures.

Install guided self-service kiosks at PTSP desks to protect pro-se citizen filing rights.

Pilot: Deploy a 90-day pilot program across two commercial hubs: Central Jakarta District Court (PN Jakarta Pusat) and Surabaya District Court (PN Surabaya).

Improve: Evaluate pilot performance against established quantitative benchmarks:

Reduce docket data synchronization latency from 12-24 hours to under 5 seconds.

Achieve over 95 percent conversion of rendered decisions into valid LegalDocML XML schemas.

Attain 99.2 percent precision in automated PII redaction across published decisions.

Increase unassisted pro-se filing completion at PTSP kiosks from 22 percent to over 80 percent.

Maintain zero unauthorized database modifications during red-team security testing.

## Judicial Knowledge Takeaways

Primary Parliamentary Legislation Secures Digital Mandates: Codifying electronic procedures within formal civil procedure codes resolves constitutional challenges regarding due process and ink signature requirements.

Centralized Cloud Repositories Eliminate Synchronization Latency: Replacing decentralized local court databases with a unified cloud architecture prevents record mismatches and eliminates manual transmission overhead.

Dual-Track Filing Safeguards Pro-Se Access to Justice: Protecting the right of unrepresented citizens to submit paper filings while court staff handle digital ingestion prevents technology from disenfranchising non-technical litigants.

Machine-Readable Semantic Schemas Enable Advanced Analytics: Structuring court records into LegalDocML XML format allows automated citation validation and PII redaction without exposing confidential details to external AI models.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What statutes establish civil court digitalization in Japan? A: Civil court digitalization in Japan is established under the Code of Civil Procedure Amendment (Act No. 48 of 2022) and the Comprehensive Digitalization Integration Act of June 2023, taking full effect on May 21, 2026.

Q: What are Japan's "Three Pillars of Civil Procedure Digitalization"? A: The "3e" framework comprises e-Court (web-conferenced oral arguments and preparatory proceedings), e-Filing (mandatory online submissions via mints for attorneys), and e-Case Management (centralized cloud repository for 24/7 docket inspection).

Q: How does Japan satisfy constitutional open-court requirements during remote hearings? A: Under Article 82 of the Constitution of Japan, presiding judges sit in open courtrooms where remote video feeds of litigating counsel are projected onto public gallery monitors viewable by visitors and journalists.

Q: What is the main architectural limitation of Indonesia's SIPP database system? A: SIPP relies on decentralized, local database instances across 800+ courts that transmit daily batch updates to central servers, causing synchronization delays, packet loss in remote islands, and record mismatches.

Q: What is the proposed e-Peradilan Modern reference architecture for Indonesia? A: It is an integrated judicial IT framework that replaces local SIPP databases with a central cloud-native repository, adopts OASIS LegalDocML XML schemas, integrates Puskarda 2.0 Kafka event streaming, and automates PDP Law PII redaction.
