# Episode 01: Building Effective Court Mediation: Japan's Civil Conciliation Model for Judicial Reform

## About This Episode

In this episode, Cecep Mustafa examines Japan's civil conciliation infrastructure (Minji Chōtei) under the Civil Conciliation Act of 1951 (Act No. 222 of 1951) and evaluates its strategic value for court-annexed mediation reform in Indonesia. He explores how Japan utilizes specialized multi-member Conciliation Committees (Chōtei Iinkai) and early issue-sorting (sōten seiri) to structure disputes and facilitate negotiated settlements. The episode examines reported settlement performance, differences across court systems, and the institutional mechanisms that contribute to effective court-connected conciliation. Cecep Mustafa then analyzes potential operational bottlenecks in single-mediator models under Supreme Court Regulation (PERMA) No. 1 of 2016 and presents a structured 30-60-90 day pilot framework for Indonesian courts. The proposed approach adapts relevant Japanese institutional practices while emphasizing multidisciplinary expertise, early issue identification, settlement quality, measurable outcomes, and sustainable court-annexed mediation reform.

## Why This Topic Matters

Court-annexed mediation operates at the intersection of judicial administration and alternative dispute resolution. Its primary goals are reducing court backlogs, lowering litigation costs, and delivering sustainable dispute outcomes. In many jurisdictions, mandating mediation as a compulsory procedural gatekeeper fails to generate meaningful settlement when incentives, neutral skill sets, and attorney behaviors remain tied to adversarial litigation strategies. Analyzing Japan's high-performing conciliation framework provides actionable insights for transitioning court mediation from a routine formalistic hurdle into an effective, evaluative-facilitative dispute resolution mechanism.

## Japan Perspective

Japan's civil conciliation system (Minji Chōtei) is governed by the Civil Conciliation Act of 1951 (Act No. 222 of 1951). Historically rooted in dispute resolution practices emphasizing social harmony (wa), the system is built around specialized Conciliation Committees (Chōtei Iinkai). Each committee consists of one presiding Conciliation Judge (Chōtei Kan-an) and two or more lay Conciliation Commissioners (Chōtei Iin) appointed by the Supreme Court of Japan. Lay commissioners are selected from civil society and include attorneys, accountants, architects, engineers, medical specialists, and experienced corporate executives.

Procedurally, Japanese conciliation integrates early issue-sorting (sōten seiri) to narrow contested facts before settlement evaluations begin. Under Article 34 of Act No. 222, non-appearance without valid cause is punishable by administrative fines. When conciliation yields an agreement, Article 16 establishes that terms recorded in an official Conciliation Record (Chōtei Toshosho) carry immediate executory force equivalent to a final judicial judgment.

Empirically, Japanese civil conciliation consistently achieves high settlement rates. Official Supreme Court statistics from 2022 show that Minji Chōtei handled 33,934 national cases with an overall success rate exceeding 50 percent. Summary Court civil conciliation achieved settlement rates between 50 percent and 55 percent across 25,789 cases, while District Court conciliation achieved rates between 45 percent and 50 percent across 8,145 cases.

Technological integration has further streamlined these proceedings. Under the Code of Civil Procedure amendments (Act No. 48 of 2022) and the 2023 Integration Act (Act No. 53 of 2023), Japan established a full digital transition. This includes mandatory e-filing for licensed attorneys via the Supreme Court mints platform, centralized e-case management, and widespread use of web-conferencing software for preparatory hearings and conciliation sessions.

## Comparative Perspective

Comparative analysis reveals both shared normative goals and key structural divergences between Indonesia and Japan.

Both legal systems recognize court-connected mediation as an essential tool to manage caseloads and preserve social relationships. Indonesian mediation draws cultural legitimacy from the constitutional principles of Pancasila and the customary governance philosophy of Musyawarah Mufakat (deliberative consensus). Both jurisdictions assign full executory force to mediated agreements: Settlement Deeds (Akta Dading) in Indonesia under PERMA No. 1 of 2016 and Conciliation Records (Chōtei Toshosho) in Japan under Act No. 222 of 1951. Both have also established frameworks for electronic proceedings: PERMA No. 3 of 2022 in Indonesia and Act No. 48 of 2022 in Japan.

However, operational architectures differ significantly. Indonesia relies on a single mediator (either an active judge mediator or an external non-judge mediator certified via a 40-hour course) operating within a strict 30-calendar-day window. Under Article 7 of PERMA 1/2016, judges can declare lawsuits inadmissible (Niet Ontvankelijk Verklaard / NO) and impose cost orders for bad-faith participation (tidak beriktikad baik), though trial judges rarely enforce these sanctions in practice. In contrast, Japan uses multi-member expert committees, flexible timeframes structured around issue-sorting rounds (sōten seiri), and allows direct pre-litigation conciliation filings or ex officio court transfers.

These structural differences produce contrasting empirical outcomes:

In Indonesia, nationwide settlement rates in general civil litigation at District Courts (Pengadilan Negeri) remain between 6.8 percent and 7.42 percent (recording a 7.42 percent success rate across 18,347 cases in 2022). Religious Courts (Pengadilan Agama) achieve higher performance with a 39.85 percent national settlement rate across 63,312 cases in 2023, driven primarily by family disputes such as divorce and child custody. Significant local performance variations exist: Rangkasbitung Religious Court achieved over 61 percent success using active judge mediators, whereas Tangerang Religious Court recorded 7.04 percent success relying on uncompensated external non-judge mediators, and Sangatta Religious Court recorded 9.02 percent in contested divorce cases between 2022 and 2024.

In Japan, civil conciliation maintains consistent settlement rates above 50 percent across summary and district courts nationwide.

The primary driver of this disparity is how neutral authority and expertise are structured. In Indonesia, active judge mediators face heavy trial dockets, leaving limited time for individual mediation sessions. External non-judge mediators offer domain expertise but lack public court budget allocations to cover their fees, making them dependent on voluntary party fees and causing high roster attrition. Litigants often perceive non-judge mediators as lacking formal authority. In Japan, the Conciliation Committee combines judicial authority directly with specialized private-sector technical analysis, dividing analytical duties effectively across the panel.

Observe → Understand → Compare → Adapt → Pilot → Improve

The pathway for adapting Japanese institutional insights into Indonesian court reform follows a six-stage methodology:

Observe: Identify operational bottlenecks in Indonesia's single-mediator model, including low District Court settlement rates (7.42 percent) and resource deficits for external mediators.

Understand: Recognize that Japan's high conciliation settlement rates (>50 percent) stem from deliberate structural design choices, multidisciplinary panel composition, and early issue-sorting rather than cultural factors alone.

Compare: Contrast Indonesia's 30-day single-mediator gatekeeping rule under PERMA 1/2016 against Japan's multi-member evaluative-facilitative committees under Act No. 222 of 1951.

Adapt: Formulate specific regulatory proposals for Indonesia:

Amend PERMA 1/2016 to create Tripartite Conciliation Panels (1 Judge Mediator + 2 Certified Non-Judge Specialist Commissioners) for complex civil, land, and commercial disputes.

Codify a mandatory 10-day Issue-Sorting and Assessment Phase (Tahap Formulasi & Evaluasi Sengketa) to provide non-binding preliminary evaluations.

Enact a Joint Ministerial Decree (Surat Keputusan Bersama / SKB) between the Supreme Court, Ministry of Law, and Ministry of Finance to establish dedicated public funding and per-case honoraria for non-judge mediators.

Upgrade digital mediation capabilities under PERMA 3/2022 with secure digital evidence rooms, virtual caucuses, and electronic signature validation.

Pilot: Execute a 90-day pilot program across four representative courts: PN Jakarta Selatan (General Civil), PA Jakarta Selatan (Family/Sharia), PN Surabaya (Commercial/Land), and PA Sleman (Family/Local Wisdom).

Improve: Evaluate pilot outcomes against empirical benchmarks (target District Court settlement >=25 percent, Religious Court settlement >=55 percent, settlement duration <=18 calendar days, non-judge allocation >=45 percent, bad-faith penalty enforcement 100 percent) to refine regulations prior to national deployment.

## Judicial Knowledge Takeaways

Multidisciplinary Panels Enhance Authority and Evaluation: Combining judicial authority with private-sector technical expertise resolves domain knowledge gaps and prevents mediation sessions from becoming superficial procedural formalities.

Early Issue-Sorting Drives Settlement Realism: Introducing a structured issue-sorting phase (sōten seiri) narrows contested facts early, giving parties a clear evidentiary basis for realistic settlement evaluations.

Dedicated Public Funding Is Essential for Neutral Roster Retention: Court-annexed mediation cannot rely on voluntary party fees to compensate non-judge neutrals. Securing state budget line items is necessary to maintain professionalized rosters and protect lower-income litigants.

Judicial Authority Must Integrate into Mediation Workflows: Litigants engage more actively in negotiations when presiding judicial neutrals actively oversee procedural structure and enforce good-faith participation rules.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the statutory foundation of civil conciliation in Japan? A: Civil conciliation in Japan (Minji Chōtei) is governed by the Civil Conciliation Act of 1951 (Act No. 222 of 1951), supplemented by the Code of Civil Procedure and recent digital court transformation statutes (Act No. 48 of 2022).

Q: How do settlement rates compare between Indonesian District Courts and Japanese Civil Conciliation? A: Indonesian District Courts record nationwide mediation settlement rates between 6.8 percent and 7.42 percent. Japanese Civil Conciliation consistently achieves nationwide settlement rates exceeding 50 percent across both Summary Courts and District Courts.

Q: How is a Japanese Conciliation Committee (Chōtei Iinkai) structured? A: A Conciliation Committee consists of one presiding Conciliation Judge (Chōtei Kan-an) and two or more lay Conciliation Commissioners (Chōtei Iin) selected by the Supreme Court of Japan from legal, financial, engineering, corporate, and medical backgrounds.

Q: Why do external non-judge mediators in Indonesia face high roster attrition? A: Public court budgets in Indonesia currently lack dedicated line items to compensate external non-judge mediators. External mediators must rely on voluntary party-paid fees, creating financial hurdles for lower-income litigants and leading to high attrition among certified non-judge professionals.

Q: What are the primary proposals for adapting Japan's model in Indonesian courts? A: Recommended reforms include amending PERMA 1/2016 to establish Tripartite Conciliation Panels, codifying a mandatory 10-day Issue-Sorting Phase, issuing a Joint Ministerial Decree (SKB) for state-funded non-judge mediator compensation, and upgrading PERMA 3/2022 digital mediation infrastructure.
