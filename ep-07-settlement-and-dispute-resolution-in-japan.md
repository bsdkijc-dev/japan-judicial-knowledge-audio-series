## About This Episode

In this episode, Cecep Mustafa examines Japan's court-annexed civil conciliation (Chōtei) framework under the Civil Conciliation Act (Act No. 222 of 1951) and the Domestic Relations Case Procedure Act (Act No. 52 of 2011). He explores how Japan utilizes multidisciplinary Conciliation Committees (Chōtei Iinkai) and judicial Orders in Lieu of Conciliation (Chōtei ni kawaru kettei) under Article 17 to facilitate negotiated settlements in civil and family disputes. The episode considers reported settlement outcomes in general civil and complex inheritance matters while examining the institutional mechanisms that support effective court-connected conciliation. Cecep Mustafa then contrasts the Japanese framework with Indonesia's PERMA No. 1 of 2016 and presents a structured 90-day pilot model for Indonesian trial courts. The proposed model introduces Tripartite Conciliation Panels (Majelis Konsiliasi Tripartit) and Orders in Lieu of Settlement (Penetapan Pengganti Perdamaian), with a focus on multidisciplinary dispute resolution, judicial oversight, settlement quality, procedural efficiency, and access to justice.

## Why This Topic Matters

Court-connected mediation is vital for alleviating trial docket congestion and promoting consensual dispute resolution. However, when mediation operates under a solo-mediator model lacking domain expertise or evaluative authority, proceedings often degenerate into formalistic administrative hurdles. In Indonesian general civil courts (Pengadilan Negeri), mediation success rates hover between 4.0 percent and 4.08 percent. Analyzing Japan's hybrid conciliation architecture offers actionable insights for equipping court neutrals with evaluative settlement tools and multi-disciplinary expert panels.

## Japan Perspective

Japan's civil conciliation system is governed by the Civil Conciliation Act (Minji Chōtei Hō, Act No. 222 of 1951) and the Domestic Relations Case Procedure Act (Kaji Jiken Tetsuzuki Hō, Act No. 52 of 2011). Rather than relying on isolated judicial officers, conciliation is conducted by a Tripartite Conciliation Committee (Chōtei Iinkai) comprising one career judge (the Chief Conciliator) and two lay Civil Conciliation Commissioners (Minji Chōtei-in). These commissioners are appointed by the Supreme Court of Japan from professional backgrounds, including attorneys, certified public accountants, real estate appraisers, architects, and experienced community leaders.

Under Article 257 of the Domestic Relations Case Procedure Act and Article 24-2 of the Civil Conciliation Act, Japan enforces Chōtei Zenchi Shugi, requiring mandatory conciliation prior to litigation in domestic relations, inheritance, and real estate rent disputes.

When negotiating parties reach a narrow impasse, Article 17 of the Civil Conciliation Act empowers the court to issue an Order in Lieu of Conciliation (Chōtei ni kawaru kettei) ex officio. After consulting the committee, the court crafts an equitable resolution balancing the parties' rights. Unless a party files an objection within a statutory two-week window, the order gains the identical legal force of a final court judgment. In domestic relations matters, an equivalent decree (Kaji Shimbai ni kawaru kettei) under Article 284 yields a 71.8 percent aggregate resolution rate in complex inheritance divisions (isan bunkatsu), combining 42.0 percent direct agreements with 29.9 percent un-objected judicial orders. Non-appearance is deterred by administrative fines up to 50,000 yen under Article 349.

## Comparative Perspective

Comparing court-connected dispute resolution between Japan and Indonesia reveals critical structural differences.

In Indonesia, court mediation is governed by Supreme Court Regulation (PERMA) No. 1 of 2016. The system relies on a solo mediator model, assigning either a single career judge mediator or a solo non-judge mediator. General civil courts (Pengadilan Negeri) record national mediation success rates between 4.0 percent and 4.08 percent. While religious courts (Pengadilan Agama) report a higher composite success rate of 39.85 percent, this figure is largely driven by uncontested divorce petition withdrawals; contentious commercial and Islamic banking claims in religious courts yield settlement rates below 15 percent. Furthermore, non-judge mediator success in courts like Tangerang Religious Court drops to 7.04 percent due to uncompensated pro-bono status and lack of state budget allowances.

Indonesian mediators lack evaluative authority to issue proposed settlement orders when parties reach a narrow deadlock. Additionally, trial judges rarely enforce procedural cost sanctions for bad-faith non-appearance under Article 7 of PERMA 1/2016, allowing dilatory tactics.

In contrast, Japan's model combines judicial authority with lay expert knowledge, providing evaluative orders that bridge negotiation gaps while maintaining strict statutory penalties for non-attendance.

Observe → Understand → Compare → Adapt → Pilot → Improve

The pathway for adapting Japanese conciliation mechanisms into Indonesian judicial practice follows a six-stage methodology:

Observe: Identify systemic causes of low mediation success in Indonesian general civil courts (~4.0 percent), including solo mediator fatigue, lack of domain expertise, and absent evaluative tools.

Understand: Recognize that Japan achieves high settlement rates (>50 percent in general civil, >70 percent in inheritance) through multi-disciplinary Conciliation Committees (Chōtei Iinkai) and non-binding Orders in Lieu of Conciliation (Chōtei ni kawaru kettei).

Compare: Contrast Indonesia's solo mediator framework under PERMA 1/2016 against Japan's tripartite conciliation panels and Article 17 evaluative orders.

Adapt: Formulate specific regulatory amendments to PERMA No. 1 of 2016 under the JPAIS framework:

Establish Tripartite Conciliation Panels (Majelis Konsiliasi Tripartit) pairing one certified Judge Mediator with two accredited Non-Judge Expert Commissioners for commercial, property, and inheritance claims exceeding Rp 500 million.

Introduce an Order in Lieu of Settlement (Penetapan Pengganti Perdamaian), allowing panels to issue a provisional settlement decree that becomes a binding Settlement Deed (Akta Perdamaian) if no party objects within 14 calendar days.

Require immediate deposit of a security fee for non-appearance before adversarial pleadings can commence.

Pilot: Deploy a 90-day pilot across six target courts: PN Jakarta Pusat, PN Surabaya, PN Medan, PA Jakarta Selatan, PA Surabaya, and PA Malang.

Improve: Evaluate pilot outcomes against established targets: increase general civil court mediation success from ~4 percent to at least 25 percent, achieve an aggregate settlement rate of at least 60 percent in religious court property cases, and reduce average mediation duration from 30 working days to 21 calendar days or fewer.

## Judicial Knowledge Takeaways

Tripartite Panels Overcome Domain Knowledge Gaps: Pairing career judges with technical experts (accountants, land valuers, industry specialists) enhances credibility and facilitates interest-based negotiation.

Evaluative Settlement Orders Bridge Impasses: Empowering panels to issue provisional settlement orders subject to a 14-day objection window resolves deadlocks without violating party autonomy.

Mandatory Attendance Sanctions Prevent Dilatory Tactics: Enforcing financial deposits for non-attendance deters litigants from delegating mediation to representatives lacking settlement authority.

Targeted Pilot Testing Validates Procedural Reform: Deploying 90-day pilots across general and specialized courts generates empirical evidence to refine regulations before nationwide rollout.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is a Japanese Conciliation Committee (Chōtei Iinkai)? A: A Conciliation Committee under Articles 5 and 6 of the Civil Conciliation Act consists of one career judge (Chief Conciliator) and two lay Civil Conciliation Commissioners (Minji Chōtei-in) appointed from specialized civil professions.

Q: What is an Order in Lieu of Conciliation (Chōtei ni kawaru kettei) under Japanese law? A: Authorized by Article 17 of the Civil Conciliation Act, it is an ex-officio judicial order framing an equitable settlement when parties reach an impasse, gaining final judgment force if no objection is lodged within two weeks.

Q: What are the national mediation success rates in Indonesian general civil courts? A: Empirical data from Indonesian general civil courts (Pengadilan Negeri) indicates national mediation success rates between 4.0 percent and 4.08 percent.

Q: How does the proposed Order in Lieu of Settlement (Penetapan Pengganti Perdamaian) function for Indonesia? A: It allows a Tripartite Conciliation Panel to issue a provisional settlement order when negotiations deadlock, which converts into an enforceable Settlement Deed (Akta Perdamaian) unless a party objects within 14 calendar days.

Q: Which courts are included in the 90-day JPAIS mediation pilot? A: The pilot covers six courts: PN Jakarta Pusat, PN Surabaya, PN Medan, PA Jakarta Selatan, PA Surabaya, and PA Malang.
