## Why This Topic Matters

Efficient court case management directly impacts judicial performance, access to justice, and public trust. A well-designed case management architecture ensures that legal disputes transition smoothly from registration to final judgment without unnecessary delays or administrative burdens. Examining Japan's centralized civil case management and front-loaded pre-trial procedures offers practical insights for courts seeking to balance rapid case turnover with thorough, high-quality adjudication.

## Japan Perspective

Japan's civil case management system is governed by the Code of Civil Procedure and the Act on Speeding Up Trials. Intake is managed by Court Clerks (Saibansho Shoki-kan) within Summary Courts (Saimu Saibansho) for claims up to 1.4 million yen, or District Courts (Chihō Saibansho) for claims exceeding 1.4 million yen. Administrative court heads assign cases to single judges or three-judge benches based on statutory complexity, specialized subject matter, and judicial workload distribution algorithms.

Pre-trial procedure relies on front-loaded issue organization known as Preparatory Proceedings. Judges and legal counsel establish structured schedules to narrow disputed issues before formal evidentiary hearings occur. While the Act on Speeding Up Trials establishes a general policy benchmark to resolve first-instance civil cases within 2 years, Japanese District Courts achieve average resolution times between 8 and 12 months. Specialized cases at the Intellectual Property High Court (Chaitai-sai) average between 6.7 and 9.6 months.

Overall litigation volume in Japan remains low relative to population size, with District Courts handling approximately 216,000 total filings annually, including 146,000 civil first-instance suits. Summary Courts handle roughly 1.45 million filings per year. Systemic backlogs are prevented because court-annexed conciliation (Chotei) resolves over 50 percent of civil disputes prior to trial.

Digitalization is governed by the 2022 Code of Civil Procedure Amendment across three statutory phases: Phase 1 establishes web conferencing (primarily Microsoft Teams) for preparatory proceedings; Phase 2 mandates e-filing and digital file management; Phase 3 implements complete e-Court integration and digital records. Performance monitoring is conducted internally by the General Secretariat of the Supreme Court (Saikō Saibansho Jimu-sokyoku), evaluating judges on disposition speed, settlement ratios, appeal reversal rates, and procedural quality.

COMPARATIVE PERSPECTIVE A comparative analysis between Japan and Indonesia reveals contrasting operational philosophies and organizational models in court case management.

Both jurisdictions utilize digital case management portals and maintain high Supreme Court productivity. Indonesia operates e-Court under PERMA No. 1 of 2019 and PERMA No. 7 of 2022, alongside e-Berpadu under PERMA No. 8 of 2022. In 2024, Indonesia's Supreme Court handled 31,138 total cases, deciding 30,908 cases for a 99.26 percent clearance rate. Japan similarly maintains minimal backlogs, supported by high pre-trial settlement rates through Chotei.

However, key operational differences exist: First, Indonesia enforces a strict 5-month target for first-instance civil trials under Supreme Court Circular Letter (SEMA) No. 2 of 2014. In contrast, Japan relies on the Act on Speeding Up Trials 2-year policy benchmark, achieving 8 to 12 month average civil trial durations through intensive pre-trial issue organization.

Second, Indonesia's digital architecture operates through parallel software platforms: SIPP for case tracking, e-Court for civil filings, e-Berpadu for criminal integration, and EIS (Evaluasi Implementasi SIPP) for performance monitoring. Japan executes a statutory three-phase digital transformation amending the primary Code of Civil Procedure.

Third, performance monitoring in Indonesia relies on automated data compliance scoring through EIS v3, evaluating courts on data entry timeliness. In Japan, the General Secretariat evaluates judicial output, settlement efficacy, and substantive decision stability.

Systemic bottlenecks differ accordingly. Indonesia faces EIS data aggregation challenges, summons service delivery failures under SEMA No. 1 of 2023 registered mail protocols, and e-Berpadu manual fallback occurrences. Japan faces procedural pressure known as mediatory adjudication, where judges heavily evaluated on settlement quotas may exert pressure during conciliation, as well as legacy cultural reliance on physical documentation and name seals (Hanko).

Observe → Understand → Compare → Adapt → Pilot → Improve

The operational methodology for adapting Japanese case management insights into Indonesian court reform follows six structured stages:

Observe: Identify operational friction in Indonesian trial courts, including manual audit overhead in EIS v3, summons delivery delays under SEMA 1/2023, and lack of pre-trial issue narrowing.

Understand: Recognize that Japan's docket efficiency relies on front-loaded Preparatory Proceedings (sōten seiri), Court Clerk intake administration, and judicial evaluation tied to settlement quality rather than raw entry speed.

Compare: Contrast Indonesia's rigid 5-month SEMA 2/2014 deadline against Japan's procedural pre-trial narrowing under the Code of Civil Procedure and Act on Speeding Up Trials.

Adapt: Formulate four targeted technical and administrative reform proposals for Mahkamah Agung:

Technical upgrade to EIS 4.0 featuring granular automated anomaly detection to eliminate manual audit overhead for Supervisory Judges (Hakim Pengawas Bidang).

Integration of an address verification API connecting e-Court with Ministry of Home Affairs (Dukcapil) population records and PT Pos Indonesia logistics tracking to validate postal summonses.

Codification of a mandatory Pre-Trial Issue Formulation Conference (Musyawarah Pra-Sidang) via a new PERMA to narrow contested issues before receiving evidence.

Permanent statutory codification of e-litigation and e-Berpadu workflows into primary procedural codes (RUU KUHAP and RUU Hukum Acara Perdata).

Pilot: Deploy a 90-day pilot program across five representative courts: PN Jakarta Pusat, PN Surakarta, PN Surabaya, PN Makassar, and PN Medan.

Improve: Measure pilot performance against six quantitative Key Performance Indicators:

EIS audit time burden reduced from 8-12 hours per week to under 1.5 hours per week.

Summons delivery accuracy increased from 70-75 percent to over 92 percent.

Average first-instance civil duration reduced from 4.2-4.8 months to under 3.5 months.

e-Berpadu manual paper fallback rate reduced from 15-25 percent to under 3 percent.

Pre-trial issue narrowing rate increased from 0 percent to over 80 percent of pilot cases entering trial with a binding Order on Scope (Penetapan Lingkup Sengketa).

EIS system compliance conversion score sustained above 98.5 percent.

## Judicial Knowledge Takeaways

Front-Loaded Pre-Trial Issue Formulation Speeds Adjudication: Conducting structured pre-trial conferences to narrow legal and factual issues prevents piecemeal hearings and shortens overall trial duration.

Granular Automated Monitoring Reduces Audit Overhead: Upgrading performance platforms to flag specific non-compliant case files automatically removes manual tracking burdens from supervisory judges.

API Integration Fixes Summons Delivery Vulnerabilities: Connecting court filing portals directly with population registries and postal logistics services ensures accurate service and reduces default judgments.

Primary Legislative Codification Secures Digital Litigation: Embedding electronic court procedures into primary national procedural codes eliminates procedural challenges regarding online witness testimony and digital records.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the statutory time benchmark for civil trials in Japan? A: The Act on Speeding Up Trials sets a general policy benchmark to dispose of first-instance civil cases within 2 years, though Japanese District Courts achieve average resolution times between 8 and 12 months.

Q: How does Japan manage case intake and judicial assignment? A: Court Clerks (Saibansho Shoki-kan) manage intake in Summary Courts for claims up to 1.4 million yen and District Courts for larger claims. Administrative court heads assign cases to single judges or three-judge benches based on complexity and workload algorithms.

Q: How does Indonesia evaluate local court case management performance? A: Indonesia utilizes Evaluasi Implementasi SIPP (EIS), an automated software platform managed by Ditjen Badilum that scores local courts on data entry compliance, document completeness, and operational timeliness.

Q: What are the main features of Japan's civil digital court transformation? A: Enacted under the 2022 Code of Civil Procedure Amendment, Japan's digital transformation follows three phases: Phase 1 web conferencing for preparatory hearings, Phase 2 mandatory e-filing and digitized records, and Phase 3 full online e-Court litigation.

Q: What is the proposed Pre-Trial Issue Formulation Conference for Indonesian courts? A: It is a proposed mandatory pre-trial conference (Musyawarah Pra-Sidang) where parties present documentary evidence and arguments early, allowing judges to issue a binding Order on Scope of Trial (Penetapan Lingkup Sengketa) that limits subsequent oral hearings.
