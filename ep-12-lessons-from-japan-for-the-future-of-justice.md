## About This Episode

In this episode, Cecep Mustafa examines court performance measurement architectures, comparing Japan's macro-level statutory monitoring under the Act on Expediting Trials (Act No. 107 of 2003) with Indonesia's real-time digital monitoring through the Case Tracking Information System (SIPP) and SIPP Implementation Evaluation (EIS) platform.Listeners will explore how rigid, real-time speed metrics can trigger Goodhart's Law distortions, including premature procedural dismissals (putusan NO) and manipulation of case registration timing. Cecep Mustafa then outlines a practical 90-day reform blueprint for Indonesian courts, introducing Differentiated Case Management (DCM), a Case Weighting Matrix (CWM), and an Appellate Durability Module to promote more meaningful, balanced, and sustainable measures of judicial performance.

## Why This Topic Matters

Evaluating judicial performance requires balancing operational timeliness with substantive adjudicative quality. When administrative frameworks rely on uniform speed deadlines and real-time public league tables, court personnel face systemic pressure to prioritize transactional compliance over thorough legal reasoning. Under Goodhart's Law, when a procedural measure becomes a high-stakes evaluative target, it ceases to function as an objective metric. Analyzing Japan's flexible two-year statutory benchmark and biannual verification reports offers actionable strategies for designing balanced judicial performance scorecards that preserve adjudicative integrity.

## Japan Perspective

Japan's court performance evaluation framework is anchored in the Act on Expediting Trials (Act No. 107 of 2003). Rather than imposing real-time transactional speed rankings or monthly disposition quotas on individual judges, the Supreme Court of Japan (Saikō Saibansho) and its General Secretariat (Jimusōkyoku) evaluate systemic efficiency across a statutory two-year macro-horizon.

Systemic progress is verified through biannual empirical reviews (Saiban no Jinsokuka ni Kakawaru Kenshō Kekka no Kōhyō) and annual statistical compilations (Shihō Tōkei Nenpō). Official judicial statistics show that regular civil first-instance cases in District Courts (Chihō Saibansho) average 12.3 to 12.4 months in trial duration, labor disputes average 15.9 months, and complex evidentiary disputes reach 18.8 months.

Judicial efficiency is achieved by front-loading proceedings through intensive preparatory procedures (benron junbi tetsuzuki), which narrow legal and factual issues before trial. As a result, Japanese courts average only 2.4 formal oral argument sessions per case. Oversight is maintained through long-term career administration, collegial chamber reviews (gōgisei), and three-year judicial rotation cycles, isolating judges from real-time scorecard pressures.

COMPARATIVE PERSPECTIVE A comparative assessment highlights contrasting administrative paradigms between Japan and Indonesia.

In Indonesia, the Supreme Court (Mahkamah Agung) and the Directorate General of the General Judiciary (Ditjen Badilum) maintain a centralized digital monitoring framework. Badilum utilizes the SIPP Implementation Evaluation (EIS v3) platform and the AMPUH quality certification framework to grade District Courts (Pengadilan Negeri) on administrative compliance with Supreme Court Circular Letter (SEMA) No. 2 of 2014, which mandates a 5-month disposition deadline for first-instance cases.

This digital architecture yields exceptionally high clearance and productivity ratios, consistently reported above 97 percent to 99.54 percent across judicial tiers. However, enforcing uniform deadlines across all cases generates operational vulnerabilities:

First, unweighted speed metrics treat all lawsuits identically regardless of factual complexity. To prevent disposition clocks from running during prolonged out-of-jurisdiction summons delivery, court registries sometimes delay formal SIPP registration.

Second, when complex cases approach the 5-month cutoff without completing evidentiary hearings, benches face pressure to issue preliminary procedural dismissals (putusan Niet Ontvankelijke Verklaard / NO) for vague pleadings (obscuur libel) or misidentified parties (error in persona). This practice registers a completed disposition in SIPP, preserving court rankings while forcing litigants to refile.

Third, counter-side customer satisfaction surveys (SKM and SPAK) conducted at Integrated One-Door Service (PTSP) desks create social pressure that inflates integrity scores.

Japan avoids these distortions by evaluating disposition speed across flexible multi-year cycles, utilizing prosecutorial screening and court-annexed conciliation (minji chōtei) to filter dockets, and conducting independent consultations with local bar associations (Bengoshikai).

Observe → Understand → Compare → Adapt → Pilot → Improve

The operational methodology for adapting Japanese performance monitoring insights into Indonesian judicial administration follows six structured steps:

Observe: Identify operational metric gaming in Indonesian trial courts, including premature procedural dismissals (putusan NO) and unweighted caseload allocations driven by SEMA No. 2 of 2014 speed targets.

Understand: Recognize that Japan maintains high adjudicative quality by evaluating trial speed across a statutory two-year horizon (Act No. 107 of 2003) using biannual empirical reviews rather than real-time individual judge rankings.

Compare: Contrast Indonesia's uniform 5-month EIS speed scorecard against Japan's flexible, complexity-adjusted statutory verification framework.

Adapt: Formulate four structural reform proposals for Badilum and Mahkamah Agung:

Replace the uniform 5-month deadline with Differentiated Case Management (DCM) in SIPP, establishing Track A (Summary/Simple: 60 days), Track B (Standard: 150 days), and Track C (Complex/Multi-Party: 270 days).

Deploy a Case Weighting Matrix (CWM) in SIPP assigning point values from 0.1 for summary infractions up to 5.0 for multi-party commercial disputes, capping active judicial workloads at 40 weighted points.

Incorporate an Appellate Durability Module into AMPUH and EIS v4 to track Appellate Affirmance Rates (AAR) and procedural dismissal ratios.

Decouple public satisfaction monitoring by migrating surveys to an automated, blind platform (ICUX) managed independently by the Supervisory Agency (Bawas).

Pilot: Launch a 90-day pilot project across representative courts: PN Jakarta Pusat (commercial), PN Sleman (standard), and PN Kupang (dispersed jurisdiction).

Improve: Evaluate pilot outcomes against established benchmarks: maintain Track Milestone Timeliness Index at 88 percent or higher, achieve an Appellate Affirmance Rate of 85 percent or higher, reduce procedural dismissals (putusan NO) below 5 percent of civil dispositions, and maintain zero local IP survey entries.

## Judicial Knowledge Takeaways

Differentiated Case Management Prevents Premature Dismissals: Categorizing lawsuits into distinct complexity tracks ensures complex cases receive adequate hearing time without triggering administrative speed penalties.

Weighted Caseload Systems Balance Judicial Workloads: Assigning credit points based on substantive case difficulty enables court leadership to distribute dockets equitably among judges.

Appellate Durability Metrics Protect Substantive Reasoning: Tracking appellate affirmance rates aligns judicial incentives toward legal durability rather than hasty administrative closure.

Independent Blind Surveys Provide Reliable Integrity Data: Delivering satisfaction evaluations via automated post-disposition channels eliminates in-person survey steering and generates objective user feedback.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the statutory foundation for court performance evaluation in Japan? A: Court performance evaluation in Japan is governed by the Act on Expediting Trials (Act No. 107 of 2003), which establishes a general statutory target of resolving first-instance civil cases within two years.

Q: How does Japan verify court disposition speeds? A: The Supreme Court General Secretariat (Jimusōkyoku) publishes biannual verification reports (Saiban no Jinsokuka ni Kakawaru Kenshō Kekka no Kōhyō) and annual statistical reports (Shihō Tōkei Nenpō) analyzing trial duration trends across courts.

Q: What is the main administrative limitation of Indonesia's EIS platform? A: The EIS platform enforces a uniform 5-month disposition deadline under SEMA No. 2 of 2014 without weighting case complexity, incentivizing premature procedural dismissals (putusan NO) to protect court rankings.

Q: What is the proposed Differentiated Case Management (DCM) system for SIPP? A: DCM is a proposed SIPP module that classifies cases at registration into Track A (Summary: 60 days), Track B (Standard: 150 days), and Track C (Complex: 270 days), scoring compliance against track-specific milestones.

Q: What is the proposed Case Weighting Matrix (CWM) for judicial allocations? A: CWM is a proposed automated module that assigns credit points to lawsuits based on complexity (from 0.1 for simple infractions to 5.0 for multi-party commercial claims) to balance workloads across judicial chambers.
